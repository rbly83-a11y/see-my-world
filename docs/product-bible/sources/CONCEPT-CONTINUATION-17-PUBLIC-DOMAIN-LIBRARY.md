This may be one of the strongest customer-acquisition strategies we've developed.

Instead of greeting new users with an empty profile or asking them to subscribe before they understand the product, give everyone an entire library of extraordinary fictional Worlds they can enter for free.

Imagine opening SEE MY WORLD for the first time and choosing to explore Wonderland, Dracula's castle, Sherlock Holmes's London, the Nautilus, or the ancient Greek Underworld.

These aren't merely free ebooks. They're interactive environments containing characters, storylines, locations, mysteries, guided expeditions, and opportunities to meet other readers.

The strategy is to make the free experience genuinely worthwhile, then introduce paid creator and franchise Worlds as a natural extension of the reader's curiosity.

Our principle should be: The classics bring readers through the door. Living authors give them new Worlds to discover.

The free library should never feel like a crippled demonstration. It needs to prove that SEE MY WORLD can make literature feel alive.

## 171. Copyright eligibility: What we can legally build from

For a U.S.-first launch, the broad rule is that works published in 1930 or earlier have entered the U.S. public domain as of 2026, subject to verifying the particular edition, source material, and applicable legal circumstances. Duke Law confirms that works from 1930 entered on January 1, 2026. [Source debris: "Public Domain Center +1"]

That gives us a remarkable library to work with.

But there are four important limitations.

* A public-domain novel does not make every later film adaptation, illustration, translation, or character redesign free to use.
* Trademark law can still matter, particularly in branding and suggesting official franchise affiliation.
* International copyright terms differ, so a U.S.-cleared World is not automatically cleared worldwide.
* Some modern editions contain protected annotations, translations, and other newly created material.

The U.S. Copyright Office explains the distinction between public-domain works and protected adaptations. [Source debris: "U.S. Copyright Office +1"]

I recommend creating an automated Public-Domain Rights Registry backed by human-reviewed source records. It should identify the exact edition, original publication date, source jurisdiction, rights status, and any restrictions on included assets.

## 172. The first free Worlds: A curated launch library

Rather than importing thousands of books and producing shallow AI-generated maps, I recommend launching with a small collection of deeply developed Worlds.

These are the strongest candidates for an initial U.S. library.

**1. Alice's Adventures in Wonderland — Wonderland.** Lewis Carroll · 1865 · Fantasy. An interactive rabbit hole, the Mad Tea Party, the Queen's garden, and logic-based adventures. *Gateway: Modern fantasy, magical academies, surreal fiction, and creator-built dream worlds.*

**2. Dracula — Dracula's Domain.** Bram Stoker · 1897 · Gothic horror. Explore Transylvania, Victorian London, diaries, correspondence, and a chronological vampire investigation. *Gateway: Contemporary vampire fiction, gothic romance, supernatural horror, and potentially licensed Anne Rice Worlds.*

**3. The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes — Baker Street.** Arthur Conan Doyle · 1892 · Mystery. Visit 221B Baker Street, examine evidence, and solve mysteries using clues from the original stories. *Gateway: Modern detective fiction, thrillers, crime dramas, and author-created mystery Worlds.*

**4. Frankenstein — The Creation Laboratory.** Mary Shelley · 1818 · Gothic science fiction. Explore the laboratory, the creature's journey, and interactive ethical questions about creation and responsibility. *Gateway: Contemporary science fiction, philosophical horror, AI fiction, and speculative literature.*

**5. Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Seas — The Nautilus.** Jules Verne · 1869–1870 · Adventure and science fiction. Tour Captain Nemo's submarine, follow its voyages, and discover marine science alongside the fiction. *Gateway: Exploration fiction, steampunk, ocean science, and modern adventure series.*

**6. The Wonderful Wizard of Oz — The Land of Oz.** L. Frank Baum · 1900 · Fantasy adventure. Travel the Yellow Brick Road, explore the Emerald City, and discover characters through the original novel. *Gateway: Magical fantasy, family-friendly adventures, and original creator universes.*

**7. The Time Machine — The Time Traveller's World.** H. G. Wells · 1895 · Science fiction. Navigate eras, explore future societies, and examine the novel's scientific and social questions. *Gateway: Time-travel fiction, dystopian literature, and speculative science.*

**8. The Odyssey — The Mythic Mediterranean.** Homer · Ancient epic · Mythology. Follow Odysseus's journey, visit mythological locations, and explore the gods, creatures, and cultural traditions of the epic. *Gateway: Modern mythology-based fiction, dark fantasy, and divine-universe stories.*

Project Gutenberg provides free digital source texts for these kinds of works and identifies many of them as U.S. public domain. It is an excellent source-discovery and ingestion starting point, though its distribution terms and source editions should still be checked. [Source debris: "Project Gutenberg +1"]

The public-domain library should not be limited to nineteenth-century classics.

A particularly interesting opportunity is to launch an annual collection of newly available literature.

It could create recurring organic discovery and press opportunities without depending entirely on paid franchise licensing.

## 174. The bridge from free classics to modern authors

The commercial opportunity is not simply placing advertisements for paid Worlds inside free ones.

It is creating meaningful literary connections.

### The Literary Discovery Bridge — Five discovery corridors

| Free entry World | Reader interest | Potential paid destination |
|---|---|---|
| Dracula | Vampires and gothic mythology | Licensed Anne Rice universe |
| Frankenstein | Creation, consciousness, responsibility | Contemporary science-fiction and philosophical-horror authors |
| Sherlock Holmes | Investigation and hidden connections | Modern detective and thriller universes |
| The Odyssey | Mythology, gods, epic journeys | Modern mythology-based literary Worlds |
| The Time Machine | Time travel and speculative futures | Contemporary science-fiction universes |

These are thematic connections, not shared canon or existing commercial partnerships.

A reader should see why a recommendation is relevant: shared genre, literary influence, subject matter, or an editorially documented relationship.

The recommendation engine should use the reader's current, explicitly chosen exploration context—not secretly mine their private Worldprint for commercial targeting.

## 175. Free World feature design

I recommend making each free World a substantial experience.

### Free World feature package

* **Explore** — Interactive locations and maps.
* **Read** — Original public-domain text.
* **Discover** — Guided story Expeditions.
* **Interact** — Bounded character dialogue.
* **Remember** — Private Reader Passport.
* **Attend** — Free scheduled literary events.

The most expensive capabilities, particularly generative character dialogue, can have reasonable usage limits on the free plan. A source-backed scripted character interaction should remain available when those limits are reached.

Paid Worlds should differentiate through their original stories, author participation, exclusive events, licensed media, and richer experiences—not by making the free classics deliberately frustrating.

## 176. A particularly valuable expansion: The Literary Genealogy Engine

This is the feature I would investigate most seriously.

Imagine a reader exploring Dracula and opening a visual map showing how vampire literature evolved.

The system could connect older folklore, nineteenth-century gothic literature, twentieth-century developments, and modern interpretations.

It would distinguish historical influence supported by sources from mere thematic resemblance.

### Example: The evolution of vampire fiction

* **The Vampyre (1819)** — An early influential literary vampire story.
* **Carmilla (1872)** — Gothic vampire fiction with intimacy, secrecy, and psychological tension.
* **Dracula (1897)** — A major landmark in the development of modern vampire literature.
* **Interview with the Vampire (1976)** — A later exploration of vampire identity and interior experience, available as an interactive destination only under appropriate licensing.

The Literary Genealogy Engine could be much broader than vampire fiction.

It could map the evolution of detective stories, science fiction, fantasy, cosmic horror, mythology-based fiction, and philosophical literature.

The resulting network would give SEE MY WORLD a distinctive discovery capability: the ability to travel through the history of an idea, from its earliest literary expressions to contemporary authors.

## 177. The free library as an acquisition engine

We need to measure whether free Literary Worlds actually lead to sustained use and paid subscriptions.

Here is a hypothetical funnel for a monthly cohort of 100,000 new visitors.

### Illustrative reader acquisition funnel

| Stage | Rate | Count |
|---|---|---|
| Visitors | — | 100,000 |
| Visitors who explore a World | 40% | 40,000 |
| Explorers who create accounts | 35% | 14,000 |
| Account holders who subscribe | 8% | 1,120 |

Illustrative assumptions only, not market forecasts. The stages are conditional, and paid conversion depends on licensed inventory, pricing, product quality, and other factors.

I would track return visits, completed Expeditions, voluntary Seed saves, Reader Passport creation, paid World previews, subscriptions, cancellations, and creator revenue.

The central question is not simply how many people we can persuade to pay. It is whether free literary exploration creates enough ongoing value that people naturally want to explore additional Worlds.

## 178. The Public-Domain World Factory

To scale beyond a handful of flagship Worlds, we need a repeatable production pipeline.

* **Rights verification** — Confirm the exact source edition and intended markets.
* **Source ingestion** — Import structured text and preserve chapter references.
* **Storyworld extraction** — Identify locations, characters, events, objects, and relationships.
* **Canon verification** — Review extracted facts and unresolved interpretations.
* **Experience generation** — Build maps, expeditions, and approved character interactions.
* **Editorial quality assurance** — Test navigation, accessibility, source fidelity, and content suitability.
* **Publication** — Release the free World with clear source attribution and edition information.
* **Discovery connections** — Add relevant, authorized pathways to other free or paid Worlds.

### A useful architectural addition

Introduce a SourceEdition entity so that a World never vaguely claims to represent an author's entire body of work.

```ts
interface SourceEdition {
  editionId: string;
  workId: string;
  title: string;
  author: string;
  originalPublicationYear: number;
  sourceUri: string;
  sourceDigest: string;
  language: string;
  translator?: string;
  illustrator?: string;
  rightsStatus:
    | "VERIFIED_US_PUBLIC_DOMAIN"
    | "VERIFIED_LICENSED"
    | "REVIEW_REQUIRED";
  permittedMarkets: string[];
  verificationRecordId: string;
}
```

The Storyworld Engine should generate only from verified source editions.

A later adaptation should be a separate source with its own rights record.

## 179. Prioritized launch recommendation

I would divide the library into three releases.

| Release | Worlds | Primary objective |
|---|---|---|
| Launch | Wonderland, Dracula, Sherlock Holmes | Prove fantasy, horror, and mystery exploration |
| Expansion | Frankenstein, Oz, The Nautilus | Broaden genre appeal and guided discovery |
| Literary Network | The Odyssey, The Time Machine, selected 1930 works | Establish cross-World connections and recurring releases |

The first three are especially useful because they require different interactive mechanics.

Wonderland tests imaginative environments and nonlinear navigation. Dracula tests documents, timelines, and gothic atmosphere. Sherlock Holmes tests evidence handling and reader participation.

If the engine can deliver those three convincingly, it has a strong foundation for many other genres.

### What I would not build initially

I would defer massive 3D open worlds, unrestricted AI character roleplay, thousands of automatically published books, and complex subscription bundles.

Those additions increase cost and operational risk before we know whether readers value the core experience.

## 180. The long-term opportunity: A living library of human imagination

The Public-Domain World Library could become more than a free-plan acquisition strategy.

It could serve as a cultural and educational foundation for the entire network.

Readers could move from ancient mythology to gothic fiction, from early science fiction to contemporary speculative literature, or from classic detective stories to modern mystery universes.

Each connection would be an invitation, not a forced recommendation.

And every year, newly public-domain works could expand the library.

### Recommended Product Bible decision

I recommend formally establishing The Free Worlds Initiative as a permanent pillar of SEE MY WORLD, with four governing rules:

* Public-domain Worlds remain meaningfully accessible without payment.
* Every World identifies its source edition, rights status, and canon boundaries.
* Recommendations to paid Worlds are transparent, relevant, and optional.
* Reader Passports and personal Worlds remain private and portable according to their respective permissions.

The strategic combination is unusually promising:

Public-domain literature supplies the foundation. The Storyworld Engine creates the experience. Creator subscriptions fund new Worlds. The Literary Genealogy Engine connects generations of storytelling.

The next development phase should turn this into a production-ready Free Worlds Library Blueprint, with detailed World templates, ingestion requirements, per-title experience specifications, editorial acceptance tests, licensing controls, and a realistic cost model.

If executed well, SEE MY WORLD would offer something much more memorable than a free trial:

A free passport into the world's greatest stories—and a doorway to the next generation of storytellers.
