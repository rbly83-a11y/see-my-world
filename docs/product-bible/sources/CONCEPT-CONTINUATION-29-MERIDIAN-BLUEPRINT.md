## ONE CITY, ONE HUNDRED YEARS — The Living City Master Production Blueprint

*Pasted by Bobby, 21 November 2026. Filed verbatim (source citation debris and prototype-widget content retained and marked). Logged as entry A-30 in `EDITION-1.4-AMENDMENT-REGISTER.md` — a post-baseline amendment compiling against Edition 1.3, for the next edition when commissioned.*

---

We've spent the previous development phases designing an entire ecosystem: personal Worlds, immersive historical environments, community memories, social discovery, historical reconstruction, and time travel.

Now we're going to bring those systems together into one cohesive, buildable product experience.

This is the transition from conceptual architecture to production design.

Our flagship will be a fictional American downtown district that visitors can explore across five historical periods.

### The Meridian District

Proposed fictional pilot setting. Its architecture and cultural details will be researched, but it will not be presented as a real historical town.

**Primary street:** Mercer Avenue. **Timeline:** 1925–2025. **First platform:** Mobile + Web.

The pilot will demonstrate something that ordinary social networks, historical websites, and virtual tours rarely combine:

A visitor can travel through the same location across generations, encounter historical evidence, explore interactive environments, and discover memories contributed by real people—all without confusing those different sources of information.

## 381. Establishing the Meridian District

Meridian is a fictional, historically inspired American community. Its geography and businesses are invented for the prototype.

This gives the production team freedom to test interactions without fabricating the history of an actual town.

### The district's five historical states

| Year | District identity | Primary experience |
|---|---|---|
| 1925 | Commercial main street | Streetcars, shops, early cinema |
| 1955 | Mid-century downtown | Theater, diner, postwar retail |
| 1985 | Changing commercial center | Music, arcade culture, retail |
| 1995 | Analog-to-digital transition | Video rental, arcade, landlines |
| 2025 | Contemporary district | Adaptive reuse, digital services |

These are creative scenario assumptions. Each period's material culture will still require historical research.

The district will initially contain one main street, three detailed venues, a public square, and several exterior-only buildings.

## 385. Venue A — The Century Theater

The theater becomes our strongest demonstration of architectural continuity.

The visitor can explore how the same building's function and appearance evolve over time.

### Interactive experience

In 1925, visitors investigate silent-film exhibition and early cinema culture.

In 1955, they examine moviegoing, ticketing, advertising, and the social role of the theater.

In 1995, they encounter a fictional discount cinema operating within the aging building.

In 2025, they explore an adaptive-reuse arts venue.

### Signature mission

**The Building That Wouldn't Disappear.** The visitor investigates how a single structure survived a century of changing commercial and cultural conditions.

The mission uses fictional venue records alongside source-backed explanations of broader historical practices.

## 386. Venue B — The Electric Arcade

This site changes dramatically over time.

It begins as a hardware store, later becomes an appliance retailer, then an arcade, and finally a contemporary community studio.

### Signature mission

**Insert Coin to Continue.** The visitor explores the arcade, interacts with original period-inspired mini-games, and discovers how public gaming culture changed.

The 1995 version also includes a Memory Hotspot where approved community contributions about arcade experiences can appear.

Actual commercial arcade games should only be included with appropriate rights. The initial prototype should use original games and fictional branding.

## 387. Venue C — The Changing Storefront

This venue demonstrates how retail changes across generations.

The visitor can compare a general store, department store, music retailer, video rental shop, and contemporary mixed-use storefront.

### Signature mission

**The Price of Progress.** The visitor investigates how people discovered, purchased, rented, and consumed goods and entertainment across eras.

The experience should avoid treating technological change as universally beneficial.

It should invite questions about access, affordability, employment, community life, and consumer choice.

## 388. Venue D — The Meridian Diner

The diner offers a consistent social location across decades.

Its menus, prices, music associations, technology, and clientele change over time.

### Signature mission

**A Table Across Time.** The visitor explores how everyday meals, employment, prices, and social customs evolved.

The diner can also host fictional scripted conversations illustrating period-appropriate perspectives.

Those conversations must be labeled as fictional reconstructions rather than authentic historical testimony.

## 389. The public square

The public square should serve as the district's central navigation hub.

It provides a place to change eras, open the city map, access Memory Walks, and review saved discoveries.

*[Source note: the original's "Meridian Square · Visitor Hub" widget (tabs: Time / Places / Memories) reads: "Open the Time Navigator and switch between five authored historical states."]*

The square should also contain an always-accessible Reality Lens.

Visitors must be able to determine whether a scene is fictional, historically documented, or interpretive.

## 390. The first complete visitor journey

The flagship experience should have a narrative arc rather than feel like a collection of unrelated exhibits.

I recommend a 25–40-minute guided experience, with unrestricted exploration available afterward.

### Mission: The Street That Remembered

* **Arrival — 2025:** The visitor enters Meridian Square and receives a historical investigation: discover how Mercer Avenue changed over a century.
* **First Jump — 1925:** Visit the cinema, inspect period transportation, and examine the district's early commercial life.
* **Second Jump — 1955:** Return to the theater, visit the diner, and compare the same buildings and sites.
* **Third Jump — 1985:** Discover the arcade, examine changing retail, and investigate how new technologies shaped social life.
* **Fourth Jump — 1995:** Enter the video rental store, explore community Memory Hotspots, and complete an analog communication challenge.
* **Return — 2025:** Compare the historical states, examine what changed, and assemble a personal reflection using saved evidence.

The visitor's decisions affect their investigation, discoveries, and personal Passport—not the established historical state of the World.

## 391. The Memory Wall integration

Every major venue should contain a contextual Memory Wall.

For the fictional Meridian pilot, the wall initially uses clearly identified fictional demonstration memories.

When a real district is eventually introduced, its public wall can accept approved contributions from actual people.

*[Source note: the original's "1995 · Arcade Memory Wall" widget (tabs: All / Photos / Stories / Music) shows three demonstration records — "The Saturday Arcade" (illustrative photograph and fictional recollection), "My First High Score" (fictional memory about meeting friends after school), "The Songs Outside the Arcade" (example music associations and contextual stories) — marked "Demonstration records only. No actual community content is connected."]*

An essential design decision: community contributions about real places should not be attached to fictional Meridian sites as though those places were identical.

They may appear in a separate comparative collection, clearly labeled with their actual context and contributor permissions.

## 395. The runtime event architecture

A visitor changing years triggers multiple systems.

The Time-State Resolver selects the scene. The authorization layer checks access. The runtime loads the appropriate assets. The Memory Service supplies eligible public projections. The Passport records the visitor's authorized progress.

```ts
interface CityVisitState {
  sessionId: string;
  cityId: string;
  selectedEraId: string;
  currentSceneId: string;
  completedMissionIds: string[];
  inspectedArtifactIds: string[];
  privateNoteRefs: string[];
  experienceRevision: number;
}
type CityRuntimeEvent =
  | { type: "ERA_CHANGED"; eraId: string }
  | { type: "SCENE_ENTERED"; sceneId: string }
  | { type: "ARTIFACT_INSPECTED"; artifactId: string }
  | { type: "MISSION_COMPLETED"; missionId: string }
  | { type: "MEMORY_REFERENCE_SAVED"; publicationId: string };
```

These events should not be treated as permission grants.

Every protected asset and contribution must remain subject to current authorization.

Visitor progress and private notes should also have independent retention and deletion controls.

In particular, submitting a memory must not automatically publish it.

## 397. The build sequence

I recommend six implementation sprints, with durations determined after engineering estimates.

*[Source note: the original's "Meridian · Build Order" lists:]*

* **Sprint 1 — City Foundation:** Site registry, temporal states, district navigation, and five era selections.
* **Sprint 2 — Scene Runtime:** 2.5D environments, transitions, asset loading, and accessible navigation.
* **Sprint 3 — Living Artifacts:** Interactive objects, evidence records, and historical context.
* **Sprint 4 — Visitor Continuity:** Missions, Passport progress, private notes, and session recovery.
* **Sprint 5 — Memory Network:** Private contributions, publication review, public projections, and contextual discovery.
* **Sprint 6 — Integration and Validation:** End-to-end testing, privacy review, accessibility testing, and pilot preparation.

The critical dependency is the authorization and publication system.

It should be designed during Sprint 1 even though the public contribution interface is introduced later.

Otherwise, the team risks building an immersive experience that cannot safely accommodate real user memories.

## 398. The pilot success criteria

The pilot should be evaluated against measurable goals.

The following are proposed targets for an initial usability study, not observed performance.

| Measure | Proposed pilot target |
|---|---|
| Complete first historical mission | At least 80% of participants |
| Successfully change eras | At least 95% |
| Distinguish evidence from reconstruction | At least 90% |
| Correctly identify memory publication scope | At least 95% |
| Complete essential tasks using accessible text | 100% of defined tasks |
| Unauthorized private-memory exposure | Zero tolerated |
| Critical consent or withdrawal defects | Zero tolerated |

Engagement metrics such as session length and return visits should be secondary to comprehension, user trust, and meaningful exploration.

## 399. The principal production risks

There are four risks that could derail this project if we ignore them.

**Scope explosion.** Building five fully explorable historical cities before validating one street would consume enormous resources. Meridian must remain intentionally small.

**False historical authority.** Beautiful reconstructions can make speculation appear factual. The Reality Lens and provenance requirements must be built into the scene system from the beginning.

**Social moderation costs.** Real photographs, memories, and community disputes require ongoing review. The first public pilot should use controlled enrollment and limited contribution volume.

**Immersive production economics.** Custom 3D scenes, licensed music, and generated reconstructions can become expensive. Reusable assets, documentary-first Portals, and 2.5D environments provide a more practical starting point.

The most dangerous mistake would be building an impressive time machine before proving that users trust the memory network.

## 400. Product Bible decision — Meridian becomes the reference implementation

*[Source note: the original's "MERIDIAN · Reference Experience" summary reads: Experience — One City, One Hundred Years; Historical states — 5; Detailed venues — 3; Primary technology — Web + Mobile 2.5D.]*

Meridian should become the reference implementation for all future Living Cities.

Its components must be reusable. A future Buffalo district, Florence reconstruction, or community-built neighborhood should use the same Time-State Resolver, Scene Runtime, Memory Network, Rights Ledger, and Reality Lens.

The historical content will differ. The underlying architecture should not need to be reinvented.

The next phase should define the actual screen-by-screen visitor experience, complete mission logic, scene transition states, artifact behaviors, accessibility flows, backend response contracts, and test fixtures.

It should also establish a production readiness gate: what must exist, what must be tested, and what must be proven before the project moves from a detailed Product Bible into engineering implementation.

The long-term ambition remains enormous, but the first tangible proof can be beautifully simple:

A person stands on one street, changes the year, discovers how the place evolved, and finds a doorway into the memories of people who experienced a different time.

One street becomes five eras. Five eras become a hundred years. And a hundred years becomes a World that people can continue building together.
